Appendix: calculation details

All numbers, figures and decisions in the main text are calculated with the equations and procedures below. The embedded simulator (JavaScript) and the Python code that produced the figures use the same equations and values. This appendix alone is written to be sufficient to reproduce the entire calculation.

A2. Overall structure of the calculation and the decisions

The model does not follow time; it finds the equilibrium state under a given Δσ. The equilibrium state is the state in which three quantities, the GUV's aspect ratio k, the membrane tension τ and the relative increase in membrane area α, satisfy the following three relations at the same time.

Relation (1): shape and stress (A3–A5)
ln⁡k=Φν(s),s=15(1−ν)Δσ2(7−5ν)G+4(4−5ν)Gin+(17−19ν)τ/R

The shape the GUV takes under tissue stress Δσ and membrane tension τ. s is the quantity that equals ln⁡k for small deformations, and Φν is the mapping between s and ln⁡k including large deformations (A5). With the default Gin=0, this is the equation of 3.2.

Relation (2): shape and area (A6)
α=αshape(k)

The area increase when the GUV becomes an oblate spheroid of aspect ratio k at constant volume. It is determined by geometry alone.

Relation (3): area and tension (A7)
α=αmem(τ)

The area increase the membrane can supply under tension τ (the sum of the area released by smoothing out undulations and the area gained by stretching). It is determined by the membrane's properties alone.

Relations (2) and (3) express the same α from the side of the shape and from the side of the membrane. The GUV membrane is a single closed membrane, and we assume that no lipid enters or leaves it during deformation. The area increase required by the shape, αshape(k), must therefore equal the area increase the membrane supplies through tension, αmem(τ). This is the same reasoning as in the analysis of micropipette experiments, where the apparent area change is obtained from the vesicle geometry and related to tension (Evans & Rawicz 1990).

Stress required for a given tension (the flow in Fig. 6). From relations (2) and (3), the shape for a tension τ is k(τ)=αshape−1(αmem(τ)). Substituting it into relation (1), the stress needed to produce that tension is:

Δσneeded(τ)=Φν−1(ln⁡k(τ))[2(7−5ν)G+4(4−5ν)Gin+(17−19ν)τ/R]15(1−ν)

Monotonicity. αmem(τ) increases monotonically with τ (both terms of A7 increase), and αshape(k) increases monotonically for k≥1, so k(τ) increases monotonically. Φν is monotonically increasing, so Φν−1 is too, and the bracketed term also increases with τ. Hence Δσneeded(τ) increases monotonically with τ, and the equilibrium tension for a given Δσ is unique.

A3. Deformation of a GUV in tissue: Eshelby's equivalent-inclusion method

Far-field stress and strain: axis 3 points in the tumor's radial direction, and axes 1 and 2 lie along the tumor surface. The far-field deviatoric stress and the corresponding strain from isotropic linear elasticity are:

σ∞=diag(Δσ3,Δσ3,−2Δσ3),ε0=(1+ν)σ∞−νtr(σ∞)IE

The isotropic part is omitted because, for a constant-volume GUV, it only changes the internal pressure and not the shape.

Equivalent-inclusion equation: the GUV is treated as a spheroidal inclusion with zero shear modulus and a very large bulk modulus. Numerically, we use G*=10−7G and K*=105K as this limit (K is the bulk modulus of the matrix). Eshelby's equivalent-inclusion equation (Kato 2008, Eq. 3.24)

C*(ε0+Sε*)=C(ε0+Sε*−ε*)

is solved for the eigenstrain ε* to obtain the strain inside the inclusion, εin=ε0+Sε*. C and C* are the elasticity tensors of the matrix and the inclusion. In practice, we solve the linear system [(C*−C)S+C]ε*=(C−C*)ε0 written in 6×6 Mandel notation.

Eshelby tensor S: for a spheroid with semi-axes (a1,a2,a3)=(a,a,c), the surface integral over the unit sphere (Nozu 2021, Eq. 33) is evaluated numerically in the form:

Sijkl=116π(1−ν)∮λigjkl+λjgiklgdΩ
λi=liai2,g=∑mlm2am2,gjkl=(1−2ν)(δjkll+δjllk−δkllj)+3ljlkll

l is a unit direction vector on the sphere. We use 400 Gauss–Legendre points in cos⁡θ and 400 equally spaced points in azimuth. The result was verified against three analytical cases:

  • Sphere: agrees with Kato 2008, Eq. 3.13 (e.g. S1111=(7−5ν)/[15(1−ν)])
  • Thin-disk limit: agrees with Kato 2008, Eq. 3.14 (e.g. S3333=1, S1313=1/2)
  • S1313 of an oblate spheroid: agrees with Nozu 2021, Eq. 58

Conversion to the aspect ratio: from the strain inside the inclusion, the semi-axes are updated as a→a(1+ε11in) and c→c(1+ε33in), and ln⁡k=ln⁡(a/c). For a spherical GUV under a small stress, ln⁡k≈15(1−ν)/(7−5ν)×Δσ/(2G), which agrees with the equation in A4 for τ→0.

A4. Closed-form equation including membrane tension and the stiffness of the contents

Style, Wettlaufer and Dufresne (2015) gave the displacement field for a droplet of radius R with surface tension γ inside an elastic solid under a far-field uniaxial strain ε (their Eqs. 4–6). Rewritten in our notation, with ρ=R/L and L=γ/E, the coefficients are:

B=(1+ν)ε3,CR3=5(1+ν)[ρ−(1+ν)]ε6D0,DR5=(1+ν)[ρ+1−ν−2ν2]εD0
D0=ρ(7−5ν)+(1+ν)(17−19ν)

With the amplitude of the second Legendre component of the radial surface displacement U2/R=2B+2(5−4ν)C/R3−3D/R5, the difference between the axial and lateral strains is 32U2/R. Our loading is the sum of a uniaxial compression Δσ along axis 3 and an isotropic part Δσ/3 (diag(0,0,−Δσ)+Δσ3I=σ∞). Extracting the change in shape with ε=Δσ/E and setting γ=τ gives the closed form:

s=15(1−ν)Δσ2(7−5ν)G+4(4−5ν)Gin+(17−19ν)τ/R

The Gin term is added from Eshelby's classical result for a spherical elastic inclusion (default 0). The equation agrees with Eq. 7 of Style et al. for ν=1/2 and with A3 for τ→0 and Gin=0. Compared with values calculated directly from the coefficients above for arbitrary ν and ρ, the difference was about 10⁻¹⁵ (rounding error).

A5. Large deformations: constructing and using Φν

Small-deformation theory gives ln⁡k≈s. For large deformations, the far-field stress is increased in small steps, S is recalculated for the GUV's current shape at each step, and the increments of ln⁡k are accumulated. We write this mapping as ln⁡k=Φν(s).

  • Integration variable: x=Δσ/(2G) (calculated with G=1; the result depends only on x).
  • Increment: for the current aspect ratio k, the A3 calculation is performed for a constant-volume spheroid with semi-axes a=k1/3 and c=k−2/3 (R=1) under a small strain (h=10−4, Δσ=2Gh), giving dln⁡k/dx=(ε11in−ε33in)/h.
  • Integration scheme: second-order Runge–Kutta (midpoint method), with steps of 0.02 in s up to s=3, where s=15(1−ν)/(7−5ν)×x.
  • Tables of Φν(s) were made for ν = 0.2, 0.3, 0.4 and 0.49. Values are interpolated linearly in s (with linear extrapolation using the slope of the last interval for s>3) and linearly between adjacent tables in ν; for ν outside the range, the nearest table is used. The inverse Φν−1 is found by bisection.

Treatment of the membrane and contents: their effects enter through the denominator of s in A4. In other words, Φν is a table for the matrix alone, and the membrane enters s as "a stiffness added to that of the matrix". Consequently, only the matrix part is updated for large deformations, and the coefficient (17−19ν) of the membrane term is the value derived for a sphere. We estimated the effect of this approximation as follows. The membrane's resistance is proportional to how fast the area grows as the shape changes. For small deformations, α≈(8/45)(ln⁡k)2, and the coefficient (17−19ν) corresponds to this rate. The actual rate dα/dln⁡k is about 1.10 times this approximation at opening (k≈1.50) and about 1.12 times at rupture (k≈1.59). Strengthening the membrane term by these factors raises the required Δσ by about 1% for opening and about 2% for rupture. Because rupture shifts more, the opening window does not narrow; it widens slightly.

A6. From shape to membrane area

When the GUV becomes an oblate spheroid of aspect ratio k at constant volume, its semi-axes are a=Rk1/3 and c=Rk−2/3. The surface area and the relative area increase are:

A=2πa2[1+1−e2eartanh(e)],e=1−1k2
αshape(k)=A4πR2−1=k2/32[1+artanh(e)k2e]−1

For example, k = 1.59 gives α = 4.1%. The inverse k(α) is found by bisection.

A7. From area to tension

αmem(τ)=kBT8πκln⁡(1+cτA0/κ1+cτ0A0/κ)+τ−τ0KA,A0=4πR2,c=124π

c=1/(24π) is the coefficient for quasi-spherical vesicles (Evans & Rawicz 1990, Eq. 3). At 5 pN/nm, the first term is about 1.6% and the second 2.5%, 4.1% in total. The inverse τ(α) is found by bisection. The smaller the initial tension τ0, the more area is stored in undulations, and the larger the deformation needed to reach a given tension.

A8. Probability model for channel opening

The open probability of one channel is the Boltzmann equation Popen(τ) of 3.4. kBT/ΔA = 0.66 pN/nm sets the width of the rise: for every 0.66 pN/nm increase in tension, the ratio of the open to the closed probability is multiplied by e.

For N channels, we assume that:

  • each channel opens and closes independently,
  • the membrane tension is uniform, so all channels feel the same tension, and
  • gating is much faster than the change in tension, so the equilibrium probability at each tension applies.

The probability that at least one channel is open is then:

Pany(τ)=1−(1−Popen(τ))N

The effective opening tension, which satisfies Pany(τopen)=q, is obtained in closed form by solving the Boltzmann equation for Popen=p*:

τopen=T1/2+kBTΔAln⁡p*1−p*,p*=1−(1−q)1/N

For q = 0.5 and N = 1,000, p* = 6.93 × 10⁻⁴ and τopen = 3.13 pN/nm. The open probability at rupture is Pany(τlys) = 0.99999.

A9. Numerical methods

  • Equilibrium in the simulator: for a given Δσ, the root of f(k)=ln⁡k−Φν(s(Δσ,τ(k))) with τ(k)=αmem−1(αshape(k)) is found by bisection (60 iterations) on [1,klys], where klys is the aspect ratio at τ=τlys. If f(klys)<0, the tension reaches the lysis tension before equilibrium, and the result is "Ruptured".
  • Inverse functions: k(α) by bisection (60 iterations) starting from [1,2] and doubling the upper bound as needed; τ(α) likewise (70 iterations) from [τ0,max⁡(2τ0,1pN/nm)]; Φν−1 likewise (60 iterations) from [0,max⁡(0.5,2ln⁡k)]. The Python version that produced the figures uses the same equations with 80 bisection iterations.
  • Simulator graph: the tension curve is drawn by calculating Δσneeded(τ) at 71 values of τ from τ0 to τlys, spaced in proportion to (i/70)1.6.
  • Figures in the main text: Δσneeded is calculated and connected at 22 points from τ = 0.001 to 7 pN/nm for Fig. 12, 12 diameters from 1 to 20 µm for Fig. 13, and 27 values of N from 1 to 10,000 for Fig. 14.

A10. How the supporting numbers in the main text were obtained

  • Margin: 40, 60 and 96 kPa divided by 4.34 kPa, giving 9.2, 13.8 and 22.1.
  • Tissue twice as stiff: with G = 20 kPa, the Δσ needed for opening is 8.1 kPa and for rupture 9.7 kPa.
  • Poisson's ratio: changing ν from 0.2 to 0.49 at fixed G multiplies the required Δσ by 1.19 for opening and 1.17 for rupture.
  • Stiffness of the contents: Gin = 100 Pa raises the required Δσ by about 0.9%.
  • Lipid: switching to SOPC (KA = 190 pN/nm, same κ) raises the required Δσ by 1.2–1.5%.
  • Osmolarity: from the condition that the internal pressure from an osmotic difference Δc balances the membrane tension, ΔcRgT=2τ/R (Rg is the gas constant). With τ = 5 pN/nm, R = 2.5 µm and T = 310 K, Δc ≈ 1.55 mM, about 0.5% of the extracellular osmolarity (about 300 mOsm).
  • Far-field strain: Δσ/E, about 0.18 at opening and 0.22 at rupture.
  • Size and channel-number figures: the diameter figure keeps the number of channels per GUV fixed, and the channel-number figure keeps the diameter at 5 µm. The minimum number of channels for opening to precede rupture, obtained by solving τopen(N)=τlys for N, is 57.8.

A11. Verification

  • The numerical Eshelby tensor agrees with the three analytical cases in A3.
  • The closed form in A4 agrees with values calculated directly from the coefficients of Style et al. to about 10⁻¹⁵.
  • The simulator (JavaScript) and the Python version give the same numbers in the main text (the Δσ needed for opening and rupture, the effective opening tension and the open probability at rupture).

Tissue is a uniform, linear elastic materialEven with tissue twice as stiff, the stress needed is 8.1 kPa for opening and 9.7 kPa for rupture, far below the interface stress
Poisson's ratio of tissue is 0.2Even at 0.49, the stress needed is only about 20% higher

The GUV sits in the extracellular matrixDirect contact with individual cells is not modeled
Rupture is represented by a single threshold (5 pN/nm)The stochastic growth of pores itself is not calculated

All uncertainties checked numerically shift the opening and rupture points together, are smaller than the margin over the interface stress (9–22×), and none of them closes the opening window.

A13. Approximations in the calculation

In addition to the assumptions in A12, the calculation uses the following approximations:

  • Only the matrix part of S is updated for large deformations; the membrane coefficient (17−19ν) keeps its spherical value (effect estimated in A5)
  • The isotropic part of the stress is omitted because it does not change the shape
  • The GUV is always a constant-volume oblate spheroid
  • kBT is evaluated at 310 K, while the membrane properties are values measured at about 18 °C
  • Channels open and close independently, much faster than the tension changes
  • The undulation term of the Evans–Rawicz equation (coefficient c=1/(24π)), derived for quasi-spherical vesicles, is applied unchanged to oblate spheroids with aspect ratios of about 1.5–1.6
  • The membrane is a single closed membrane, and no lipid enters or leaves it during deformation (the basis for equating relations (2) and (3) in A2)
