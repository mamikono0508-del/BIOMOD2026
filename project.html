PROJECT / 01

Background

01-1. What is a molecular robot?

A molecular robot is an artificial system built from biomolecules such as DNA, proteins, and lipids, which senses, processes, and acts on information in a microscopic environment.

A conventional robot is composed of three elements: a sensor that receives information from the environment, a computer that processes it, and an actuator that acts back on the environment according to the result (Fig. 1-1-1). A molecular robot reproduces this architecture at the molecular scale. Proteins that receive changes in the environment serve as the sensor, reactions based on DNA base pairing serve as information processing, and the cytoskeleton serves as the actuator (Fig. 1-1-2).

Biomolecules are themselves materials with high specificity and responsiveness. By designing and combining them, molecular robotics aims to achieve autonomous operation at a scale that is difficult to reach with synthetic materials alone.

01-2. Previous work — a molecular robot that responds to a chemical stimulus (Sato et al., 2017)

Amoeba-type molecular robots that change membrane shape using the cytoskeleton have been studied. Sato et al. [1] reported an amoeba-type molecular robot assembled solely from biomolecules — DNA, proteins, and lipids — whose deformation can be switched on and off in response to a DNA signal. The robot consists of three elements: a cell-sized giant liposome as the body, kinesin and microtubules as the actuator, and a DNA "clutch" that controls whether the motor force is transmitted to the membrane.

This work is significant in that it integrated signal input (DNA), control (the clutch), and drive (the motor) into a single compartment, realizing a molecular robot whose behavior can be controlled by a signal. According to the authors, this is the first implementation of a molecular robot of this kind.

What deserves attention here is that the input of this robot is a chemical stimulus. What causes deformation is a DNA signal supplied from outside; the robot moves on receiving a chemical sign. In this wiki, a molecular robot that responds to a chemical stimulus in this way is called a Chemoresponse Robot.

01-3. Mechanobiology — cells read force as information

In living systems, on the other hand, chemical molecules are not the only source of information for a cell. Cells receive the force applied from their surroundings as information in its own right, and determine their behavior accordingly. The field that deals with this mechanism is mechanobiology.

The way force is handled as information closely resembles the case of a chemical signal (Fig. 1-3-1). An adapter molecule on the cell membrane receives a force applied from outside, or the tension of the membrane. The mechanical stimulus received there is converted into a chemical signal and, through genes and proteins, finally gives rise to a biological response.

Consider some actual examples. Stem cells differentiate into different lineages depending on the stiffness of the substrate on which they are cultured. On a soft substrate they become neurons, and on a stiff one they become bone cells: the physical property of stiffness alone determines cell fate, with no chemical factors added [2]. Hepatic stellate cells, too, sense when their surroundings become stiff and are activated to produce fibers [3].

In every case, cells receive force as a physical quantity and convert it into a chemical signal inside the cell. A mechanical stimulus is another input, standing alongside chemical molecules.

PROJECT / 02

Problem

The molecular robots implemented so far, however, have all been Chemoresponse Robots, taking a chemical or optical signal such as DNA or light as their input.

There is no precedent for a molecular robot that operates on a mechanical stimulus — a Mechanoresponse Robot.

This difference also bears on autonomy. When a chemical signal is the input, it is an external operator who decides when to supply it. In the previous model, indeed, the DNA signal that initiates deformation cannot cross the lipid membrane, so it had to be encapsulated in advance and released inside by ultraviolet irradiation. A mechanical stimulus, by contrast, is emitted by the very environment in which the robot is placed, and can be received without an operator.

This raises the following questions:

How can a molecular robot that takes a mechanical stimulus as its input be built?

Can the mechanotransduction machinery that cells possess be reconstituted from biomolecules alone?

If that were achieved, what would molecular robots become capable of?

PROJECT / 03

Our Project

03-1. Overview of aQtSOME

aQtSOME is the amoeba-type molecular robot we are developing.

The design began by overlaying the two things seen in 01. The previous model (01-2) had a body, the GUV, and an actuator, the cytoskeleton, but it had no sensor, and relied on a DNA signal from outside to trigger deformation. Cells (01-3), on the other hand, receive force through adapter molecules on the membrane and respond by converting it into a chemical signal.

To the framework of body and actuator that the previous work assembled, we add a sensor learned from mechanobiology. This is the design of aQtSOME. With the one missing element filled in, the robot no longer needs an external operator and can run on the environment itself as its input.

The operation proceeds as follows. When the GUV is pushed into a narrow gap between cells, the membrane at its leading edge is stretched and its tension rises. MscL, embedded in the membrane, detects this tension and opens, and Ca²⁺ from the external solution flows in. The Ca²⁺ that enters activates INF2, and the activated INF2 promotes actin polymerization. The elongating actin filaments push the membrane out from within, and the leading edge enters the gap.

Membrane tension → influx of Ca²⁺ → activation of INF2 → actin polymerization → protrusion of the membrane

The sequence of mechanotransduction described in 01-3 is implemented directly as a molecular design.

Components

Body: GUV

A unilamellar liposome the size of a cell. It is the vessel that encapsulates the molecular devices and, at the same time, the place where a force received from outside is converted into membrane tension.

Sensor: MscL

A tension-gated channel from bacteria. When the tension of the membrane rises, it opens and forms a large pore that lets ions and small molecules through.

Actuator: INF2 and actin

INF2 is a formin-family protein that promotes actin polymerization, and its activity is regulated by Ca²⁺/calmodulin.

We chose Ca²⁺ to carry the signal because it is an ion present universally in living systems and used by cells themselves for signaling. By setting its concentration higher outside than inside, it flows in down the concentration gradient the moment MscL opens, and the influx itself serves as the sign. No special molecule has to be designed anew, and the natural mechanism by which INF2 is regulated by Ca²⁺ can be used as it is.

What matters is that no external operation enters this sequence at any point. Force is generated at the very site where the stimulus was received. The detailed design of each component is described on the Design page.

03-2. Comparison with the previous model — from Chemoresponse to Mechanoresponse

The cytoskeleton changes from microtubules to actin, and the driving force from kinesin sliding to INF2-driven actin polymerization. The essential difference, however, lies in the presence or absence of a sensor. The previous model has no sensor, and deformation begins with a DNA signal supplied from outside. aQtSOME, by contrast, carries MscL, which detects membrane tension, and therefore takes the environment itself as its input.

This difference appears as a difference in the mode of operation: from Chemoresponse, responding to a chemical stimulus, to Mechanoresponse, responding to a mechanical one. This is the central claim of our project.

PROJECT / 04

Goals & Progress

Fig. 4 breaks our goals down by component and shows where we currently stand.

For the sensor, we confirmed that MscL is inserted into the lipid membrane. What remains is to confirm that the channel actually opens under osmotic stress and under membrane tension.

For the actuator, we confirmed both that INF2 promotes actin polymerization and that its activity is regulated by Ca²⁺.

For aQtSOME as an integrated system, we have confirmed membrane deformation driven by actin polymerization. What remains is deformation under osmotic stress and deformation under the membrane tension generated by a narrow gap.

Three issues remain to be addressed in our future design and experimental work.

  • Ca²⁺ diffuses rapidly inside a cell-sized GUV. If the Ca²⁺ that enters at the leading edge spreads throughout the interior, polymerization will occur over the entire periphery and produce only a non-directional expansion. Whether the locality of "polymerization at the site of detection" holds must be verified experimentally.
  • How to apply a defined mechanical stimulus reproducibly, and how to quantify the resulting deformation. Control experiments must also be designed to show that the observed deformation is an active protrusion rather than a passive indentation.
  • There is no mechanism to return an activated system to the inactive state. At present the sequence of sensing a gap and extending into it cannot be repeated. This is a challenge inherited from the previous model, and how to resolve it remains open.

PROJECT / 05

Feasibility

Within the period of this project, we cannot complete aQtSOME and verify its efficacy in vivo. What we aim for, therefore, is a demonstration that the concept holds as a principle.

We anticipate two difficult points.

The first is the operating condition of the sensor. Among mechanosensitive channels, MscL requires a particularly high membrane tension to open, and it works in a range close to the tension at which a lipid membrane ruptures. The window in which the channel opens in a narrow gap but does not break the GUV is narrow, and it must be secured through the choice of lipid composition and channel mutants.

The second is maintaining the resting state. Because aQtSOME is driven by setting the external Ca²⁺ concentration higher than the internal one, INF2, calmodulin, and actin must be encapsulated at working concentrations while the interior is kept free of Ca²⁺ until a stimulus arrives. Both conditions — putting everything in, yet keeping it still — must hold at once.

We nevertheless consider the demonstration feasible, because each of the component technologies has already been reported and does not require trial and error from scratch. MscL expressed in a cell-free system and incorporated into cell-sized liposome membranes, where it responds to mechanical stimuli [8][9]; biochemical characterization of actin polymerization by INF2 [10]; and regulation of INF2 activity by Ca²⁺/calmodulin [11] have all been reported. In addition, belonging to a laboratory specializing in molecular nanotechnology allows us to spend our time on design and verification rather than on establishing basic methods.

Social implementation remains a long-term goal. What this project demonstrates is whether the principle that underlies it holds.

Potential Applications — DDS for Solid Tumors

Once a molecular robot can advance by itself using a mechanical stimulus as its cue, it gains the possibility of reaching places that previous carriers could not. Drug delivery (DDS) to solid tumors is one such example.

Technologies that encapsulate anticancer drugs in liposomes and deliver them are already used in the clinic. Their transport, however, is passive: the carriers are carried by the bloodstream and wait to accumulate around the tumor. In fact, less than 1% of the administered carriers reach the tumor [4].

Inside a tumor, cancer cells proliferate densely and the extracellular matrix fills the space between them as a mesh. The remaining gaps are only about the size of a virus particle [5], and carriers that have no means of advancing by themselves are halted near the tumor surface.

Research on carriers that move actively has begun, using bacteria as carriers [6] or micro-robots that self-propel through chemical reactions [7]. All of these, however, rely on a chemical concentration gradient or an externally applied magnetic field as their cue. A design that advances using the physical information of narrowness itself as its cue is almost without precedent.

aQtSOME senses for itself that it has been pushed into a narrow gap and extends its membrane toward that gap. Beyond the point where passive carriers are halted, delivery into the deep tumor region, where drugs have not reached, can be expected (Fig. 6-1).

The constraints are nevertheless considerable. The matrix mesh inside a tumor is on the nanometer scale, and a micrometer-sized GUV is about two orders of magnitude larger. Moreover, particles of this size injected into the bloodstream are largely filtered out by the lungs, spleen, and liver. For the time being, direct injection into the tumor is the realistic premise.

What is described here is no more than a suggestion of a possible direction for application; aQtSOME is at the stage of proof of principle.
